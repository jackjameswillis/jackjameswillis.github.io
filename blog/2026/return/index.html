Returning to Research: Lessons from the Field

Created on January 06, 2026 by Jack James Willis

2026   ·   quantization   optimization   genetic   evolution   microbe   LLM   ·   research

Introduction

When I started this blog I was certain that all would go smoothly and predictably, much in arrogance of what had happened to me before. After my last blog I found myself in a deep hole of marking, and once I was out of that hole I was far behind my cofounder in progress on our software project together. It is only around now that I have found the time to get back into research, which I have so desperately wanted to get back to, but now I have some important information from practical use of machine learning that ground what I should be researching.

But first, I feel a need to comment on the current state of generative AI.

An Argument Against The Current Techno-Optimism

In my conversations with non-technical people about the state of the GenAI market, i’ve encountered a deadly pattern, specifically of optimism. This is manifest in statements like ‘I believe that the models will get better, more efficient, and cheaper’ without any recourse on facts and reason. I challenge them on every front, but after my response I get a blank face, before the individual repeats their baseless claim. It happens with all kinds of people, stretching across fields, reflecting the ubiquity of the technology in industry, and also reflecting the total lack of understanding about the technology. No doubt that the claims of AGI and super-intelligence trigger some latent hope within them, a hope which is fair considering the deep entrenchment of the technology into their workflows. Any substantive change to their belief is likely to correlate with either painful extraction of capital from them or the degredation of the product they use.

I have a particularly bleak outlook on the future of the market, albeit one where it still succeeds and flourishes, but only in spite of the optimisim. But regardless of the totality of my argument (which I will disclose in the next section), there are some simple facts that are glaring and worrisome when taken into account.

At Davos, Jensen Huang stated that renting NVIDIA gpus has gotten more expensive, at a time when the US economy is organizing around the effort to produce as much of their technology as possible. He also says that demand has never been higher, and that it is continueing to climb.

We must consider supply side shortages in GPU production such as in memory, due to the enormous demand from GenAI. I heard recently that OpenAI have a back-order on memory equivalent to the entirety of last years production. While RAM is at all time highs now, supply will only continue to dwindle as the year continues and prices will go up.

Data center energy consumption is supported by natural gas and oil turbines (I should mention that these turbines are in short supply). Both are commodities that are sensitive to the state of geopolitics, meaning that events such as war between the US and Iran could cause a spike in prices, which in turn drives up costs not only for these GenAI companies (although it especially hits them) but for people across the globe. Higher prices in general coinciding with higher inference costs will be a very bitter pill.

The antidote is to keep GenAI free in spite of its costs. At a ‘town hall’ meeting hosted by Sam Altman, an OpenAI user wants to increase the number of GPT agents as high as possible to service their customer demand, but cannot due to inference costs. Sam states that the cost of GPT 5.2 level intelligence will go down by 100x in the next year. This is supposed to be a reassuring statement, perhaps this individual will be able to scale their agents to meet demand through OpenAI, but they fail to discuss the question of WHY OpenAI would offer such cheap inference.

Disregarding the obvious issues with speculating about massive reductions in inference costs we get onto perhaps the most serious problem. Regardless of how ‘cheap’ GPT 5.2 becomes in the future, users will have to compete for the compute with people running inference on much larger models than models we currently see. The scaling laws have not gone away, and the scaling of datacenter compute has only just started, implying that the compute demands from the models themselves have not yet reached their heights. Economically it makes sense to service the larger, better models as a priority (not forgetting that Sam Altman is about as stereotypical a capitalist as you can imagine), while charging a premium, which limits the available cloud compute for smaller models.

I’ll end this rant with a final point. One of the few things I picked up on in my years studying business as a teenager was the term ‘destroyer pricing’. What we have been seeing in the field so far is exactly this, a competition between the tech giants to see who can charge nothing for inference the longest. As time has gone on, the introduction of usage limits has become normal, even so much that you can pay subscription services with usage limits. There are no guarantees that these limits are stable, and you have no control as the customer over your compute expenditure per token besides the options provided by the supplier. In other words, if you rely on their technology, they have every right to take you for a ride. So far they seem to behave relatively fairly, usage limits for most people don’t seem too extreme. But These companies are currently operating at a loss, and are not on track to turn a profit for a while. They will someday make a profit, but at the cost of the users, and that cost will have to be massive to recuperate the current losses.

My Local AI Experience

I’ve been aware of the costs of this technology for a long time, but the reality of the situation has only hit me now I have a stake in the industry with my software project. My co-founder has heavily used Minimax M2.1 through provider opencode zen for free when we started working on this project. In fact, the foundational structure of our most recent iteration was decided via communication between him and a chatbot. Vibecoding has allowed Minimax to build our app without looking at the code ever, but has also created a dependency, which was free at the time. I knew this wouldn’t last, and warned him about the incoming price increase that was bound to happen, while I started building a local setup.

I’m no skeptic of the technology, I’ve seen first hand the usefulness of GenAI, and for that reason I felt the need to disconnect from the cloud. I started selling all the old technology I had to accumulate enough capital to purchase what I would consider as the current bare-minimum local-host system, one that can run two GTX 1080 ti GPUs, totaling to 22GB VRAM. This build process took essentially the whole of January to figure out, and was done by incremental adjustments, buying and selling various graphics cards to end up at this point. This setup lets me do agentic SWE tasks through opencode with GLM-4.7-Flash-REAP at a 4 bit quantization, but I would even consider this as a luxury. It is entirely possible to gain the benefits of GenAI for coding without doing agentic coding. An alternative approach, if particularly strapped of cash, is to pick up two 8GB GPUs (ideally RTX but a cost effective choice is the 1080), and to use something like the continue.dev vscode extension to do autocomplete, code edits, chat, and perhaps even agentic work, although I haven’t tested small models on agentic frameworks much. It is likely that small models will struggle at least for the time being on these tasks, but agents aren’t necessary for a better SWE experience.

Having said this, having local SWE agents is very useful when working on shared projects that have been developed in a GenAI first way. The slop is certainly real, and is best understood by LLMs as opposed to humans. Further, I have learned that agentic coding provides a benefit to me that is extremely valuable, which is that it saves me energy consumption. No longer having to do the SWE puzzle game of coding gives me more energy to think about other things, which I value greatly. Now having a local setup, I have a particularly visceral appreciation for the LLM sitting in the PC in the corner of my room. It costs far less to upkeep than I cost, but is also saving me from the coming tidal wave of increased inference costs.

Returning to my initial point, just as I started finishing my local setup opencode zen took away the free option of Minimax M2.1, and my cofounder immediately sent the Minimax team £30 to get access again. In the next 3 days they used £10 worth of inference credits.

What This Means For Research

Having a local setup is great, but I don’t trust companies to freely give away their models in the future. At the very least we will have to buy weights from them. Training from scratch, even for local-sized models, is done on serious GPU clusters. I’ve read multiple model cards cite 100+ H100s running for weeks to train some models I use. Accounting for the models that are often distilled from would raise this number to enourmous levels. My two 1080 ti’s don’t stand a chance. This is where distributed, gradient free training comes in. Gradient-free training means that each compute node has substantially lower memory requirements to participate in training, essentially the same requirements as inference, while distributed means that nodes can be anywhere and participate, so long as they have a good internet connection.

This is a top-down appoach, but going bottom-up will involve looking at what research I have done so far. I have experiments from this time last year that indicate that binary neural networks with no scaling (i.e. batch norm) can get decent, but not acceptible, performance on MNIST. Looking at the popular quantization methods, we can essentially say that only MLP layers in the transformer are quantized, meaning that our method needs to be mixed-precision, i.e. attention parameters should be optimized in float, while MLP parameters should be optimized in some quantized fashion. Therefore, my primary concern is testing whether or not mixed-precision is feasible. In principle there shouldn’t be an issue, but it has been inconvenient to implement in the past and so I haven’t done it. One thing that all my binary neural network experiments have missed has been biases for neurons, which together with scaling pre-activations provides a nice means of testing mixed-precision optimization.

Should trying this yield positive results, the result will likely translate into my MNIST experiments, and if so demonstrate something substantial: distributed, gradient free learning can learn performant neural networks with tens of millions of parameters. This at the very least could be taken toward on-device learning of 270m parameters transformers like the function gemma models. Extrapolating further, we could see communities of people train LLMs together for agentic workflows without relying on datacenters for compute.

Conclusions

Although I have been distracted from my research, I’m now approaching it from a better informed place, which gives me a trajectory for experiments that goes further into the future, and provides an end goal which seems feasible. I’m going to try and stay accountable by posting on the blog frequently, particularly when I have some results to show.
